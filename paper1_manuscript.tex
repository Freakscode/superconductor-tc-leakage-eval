\title{Which retrospective evaluation anticipates the performance of composition-based
superconductor $\Tc$ models on unseen chemistries? A leakage-aware audit of two SuperCon
curations}

\author{Gabriel Jaime Cardona Osorio\orcid{0009-0003-3743-8559}}

\affil{Faculty of Engineering, Instituci\'on Universitaria Pascual Bravo, Medell\'in, Colombia}

\email{gabriel.cardona@pascualbravo.edu.co}

\keywords{superconductivity, critical temperature, machine learning, materials informatics,
data leakage, cross-validation, temporal validation, clustered data}

\begin{abstract}
Composition-based models of the superconducting critical temperature are usually validated by
splitting SuperCon-derived datasets at the level of the individual row, although those datasets
are assembled from doping series of a few thousand element systems. We ask which retrospective
evaluation best anticipates performance on element systems or dates that were not observed, and
which predictive information survives once duplication, family dependence and corpus selection are
controlled. On two curations of SuperCon (\num{21263} rows in \num{3365} element systems;
\num{12336} audited rows in \num{3046} systems) holding out whole element systems raises the pooled
MAE of gradient-boosted trees and random forests by \SIrange{53}{64}{\percent} and lowers $\Rsq$ by
\numrange{0.074}{0.084}, with paired family-bootstrap intervals that exclude zero; averaged per
system the inflation is \SIrange{22}{29}{\percent}, so the gap concentrates in the large systems.
The two curations share \SI{96.6}{\percent} of their element systems and are not independent
replicates. Element identity alone explains most of the conventional $\Rsq$; of what the Magpie
descriptors add over element presence, about nine-tenths is recovered by stoichiometric fractions,
and on held-out systems the descriptors alone do not beat presence plus fractions
(\SI{+0.46}{\kelvin} \ci{0.00}{0.98} and \SI{+0.16}{\kelvin} \ci{-0.18}{0.59} MAE). Separation by
composition, element system, host elements and composition clusters defines different target
populations that are not nested. Retrospective top-\num{100} precision among corpus materials whose
systems were held out stays at \SI{87.0}{\percent} and \SI{82.4}{\percent} (\SI{78.2}{\percent}
once measured non-superconductors are admitted to the candidate pool) against fold prevalences of
\SIrange{3.6}{18.2}{\percent}. Finally, on a corpus of \num{12543} compositions dated by first
report, no retrospective protocol reproduces forward-in-time performance: random cross-validation on
pre-2010 data estimates $\Rsq = \num{0.91}$ where the realised value on later compositions is
\num{0.33}; grouped protocols are closer and leave-clusters-out is closest in $\Rsq$, but none is
within \num{0.1} at more than one cut. Row-level partitions, the audited checkpoint and a per-claim
verifier are released.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Predicting the superconducting critical temperature $\Tc$ from chemical composition alone is one
of the most-benchmarked regression tasks in materials informatics. Gradient-boosted trees and
random forests on the Hamidieh featurization~\cite{hamidieh2018} or on Magpie
descriptors~\cite{ward2016} report $\Rsq \gtrsim \num{0.92}$ and MAE below \SI{6}{\kelvin}, and
deep architectures push the same metrics further~\cite{konno2021,zeng2019,stanev2018}. Such
numbers are quoted when arguing that machine learning is ready to guide the search for new
superconductors, and models of this class have been used to propose candidates from text-mined
phase diagrams~\cite{court2020}, from Eliashberg-theory descriptors~\cite{xie2022}, and to screen
hydride families~\cite{sanna2024,wrona2024}.

They are obtained in a way that does not resemble the search. The datasets derive from SuperCon and
are assembled from \emph{doping series}: materials that share a constituent-element set and differ
in stoichiometry. A YBa$_2$Cu$_3$O$_{7-\delta}$ series contributes dozens of entries whose
compositions differ in the oxygen content and whose $\Tc$ values differ by a few kelvin. When such
a dataset is split at random, near-identical variants of every test row sit in the training set,
and the metric measures interpolation within a series the model has already seen.

That extrapolative validation is harder than interpolative validation is established. Meredig
\etal~\cite{meredig2018} showed with leave-one-cluster-out cross-validation that extrapolative
performance for materials discovery is systematically worse than the interpolative figure; Xiong
\etal~\cite{xiong2020} proposed forward cross-validation to score explorative prediction; the same
lesson is settled in cheminformatics~\cite{sheridan2013} and ecology~\cite{roberts2017}, and Kapoor
and Narayanan~\cite{kapoor2023} place it within a broader pattern of leakage-driven
irreproducibility. For superconductors specifically, grouping by the constituent-element set is
already practice: Sommer \etal~\cite{sommer2023} use it as the default validation protocol of the
3DSC benchmark, with the same Ba--Cu--O--Y example, and PhaseSelect~\cite{vasylenko2023} takes the
element set itself as the unit of analysis. We do not claim the grouping as a contribution. What
has not been measured is how much of the headline accuracy the practice removes, for which target
population, and---the question this paper is organised around---\emph{which retrospective
evaluation best predicts performance on element systems or dates not yet observed, and which
predictive information survives when duplication, family dependence and corpus selection are
controlled}.

Our contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
\item A controlled measurement of the random-versus-grouped gap on identical rows, descriptors,
      models and test budgets, on two curations of SuperCon with two featurizations, with
      family-paired uncertainty rather than row-resampled intervals, and with the overlap between the
      curations measured rather than assumed away (section~\ref{sec:results-gap}).
\item A ladder of separation levels and of nulls. Separation by normalised composition, by element
      system, by host elements and by composition clusters is compared at matched test-set size
      (section~\ref{sec:results-levels}); family-mean and element-presence nulls bound how much of the
      conventional $\Rsq$ is available from a label alone, and a presence $\to$ fractions $\to$
      descriptors ablation isolates what each representation adds as differences of losses
      (section~\ref{sec:results-ceiling}).
\item A characterisation of the corpus as clustered data---family concentration and intraclass
      correlation as descriptive quantities, with an explicitly defined estimator behind any
      effective-sample-size statement---together with an audit of the chemical unit of evaluation
      (formula parsing, duplication, cross-dataset overlap; sections~\ref{sec:audit}
      and~\ref{sec:overlap}).
\item A forward-in-time test on SuperCon dated by first report, which scores each retrospective
      protocol against the performance actually realised on later compositions
      (section~\ref{sec:temporal}), plus row-level partitions and a per-claim verifier so that the
      corrected protocols and every number in the paper can be regenerated with one command.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Data and methods}

\subsection{Datasets}
\label{sec:data}

\textbf{Dataset~A} is the UCI Superconductivity Data Set~\cite{hamidieh2018}: \num{21263} rows with
\num{81} statistical features derived from elemental properties (mean, weighted mean, geometric
mean, entropy, range and standard deviation of atomic mass, ionisation energy, atomic radius,
density, electron affinity, fusion heat, thermal conductivity and valence), accompanied by a
per-element composition table giving the formula of each entry. It is used as distributed. It
contains \num{66} rows that are exact duplicates of another row in every feature and in $\Tc$, and
\num{6093} rows (\SI{28.7}{\percent}) whose \num{81} features coincide exactly with those of another
row while $\Tc$ differs, because identical compositions yield identical composition-derived
features. We do not remove them: they are part of the object of study, and the grouping of
section~\ref{sec:families} places every such pair in the same fold.

\textbf{Dataset~B} is the SuperCon-derived list curated by Stanev \etal~\cite{stanev2018}
(\num{16414} entries). Dropping the \num{3966} entries with $\Tc \le 0$ and \num{8} whose formula
\texttt{pymatgen} cannot parse leaves \num{12440} rows (the checkpoint of the previous version of
this paper). The semantic audit of section~\ref{sec:audit} excludes \num{104} of these and corrects
\num{3}, giving the audited checkpoint used throughout: \num{12336} rows in \num{3046} element
systems, featurized with the \num{132} Magpie descriptors~\cite{ward2016} of
\texttt{matminer}~\cite{ward2018}.

\subsection{Semantic audit of the corpus}
\label{sec:audit}

The chemical unit of evaluation is the formula string, so we audited every Dataset~B formula
against the SuperCon conventions documented by Sommer \etal~\cite{sommer2023} for 3DSC, where each
element must carry an explicit coefficient and a trailing \texttt{Y} after \texttt{O} (as in
\texttt{Bi4.4Sr3.6Ca2Cu4OY}) denotes an unknown oxygen content, not yttrium. The audit
(\texttt{data/audit/semantic\_audit\_B.csv}) assigns each of the \num{12440} rows a category and a
decision. We exclude \num{104} rows: \num{75} whose oxygen content is the indeterminate \texttt{OY}
marker (\num{54} of them had been assigned to a spurious yttrium-containing system), \num{7} ending
in \texttt{O} without a coefficient, \num{19} with another element lacking a coefficient in a
multi-element formula, \num{2} unrecoverable typographical coefficients and \num{1} artefact of
string concatenation. We correct \num{3} formulas with a misplaced decimal or a duplicated site,
leaving their element system unchanged. Deuterium and tritium isotopes, atomic-percentage alloys,
fulleride and clathrate coefficients, site-notation repeated elements and the \num{760} rows that
duplicate another normalised composition are kept and flagged. No retained row changes system;
\num{17} systems disappear, \num{12} of which existed only through \texttt{OY} rows. Because
\texttt{GroupKFold} breaks ties by label order, the deterministic grouped partition nevertheless
changes for \num{9453} of the \num{12336} retained rows, so a comparison of results between the two
checkpoints includes partition noise; the comparison is reported in
section~\ref{sec:results-repro}.

The same audit applied to Dataset~A revealed a collateral defect in the distributed composition
table: \num{1927} formulas (\SI{9.1}{\percent} of the rows) end in \texttt{O} without a coefficient
and are recorded with an oxygen amount of exactly one. Dataset~A is used as distributed---its
features cannot be recomputed---but section~\ref{sec:results-gap} reports the sensitivity of the
gap to removing those rows.

\subsection{Two curations of one source}
\label{sec:overlap}

The two datasets share their ancestry in SuperCon, and the overlap is nearly complete:
\SI{95.7}{\percent} of the audited Dataset~B rows have an identical formula string in Dataset~A,
\SI{96.5}{\percent} an identical normalised composition, and \SI{96.6}{\percent} belong to an
element system present in~A (\num{2883} of the \num{3046} systems; conversely \SI{92.8}{\percent}
of the Dataset~A rows belong to a system present in~B). Agreement between the two datasets is
therefore evidence that a result is stable under a second curation and a second featurization of
the \emph{same} materials, not an independent replication. The complements are small and
unrepresentative: \num{416} Dataset~B rows in \num{163} systems absent from~A (mean $\Tc$
\SI{5.7}{\kelvin}) and \num{1535} Dataset~A rows in \num{482} systems absent from~B (mean $\Tc$
\SI{64.3}{\kelvin}, \num{683} of them with the coefficient-less oxygen of
section~\ref{sec:audit}). A cross-evaluation on these complements is reported in
section~\ref{sec:results-gap} with those cautions. The publication of origin of each row is not
present in either distribution, so an intersection by publication cannot be computed.

\subsection{Element systems as the grouping unit}
\label{sec:families}

We group rows by their \emph{element system}, the set of constituent elements irrespective of
stoichiometry: Ba$_{0.2}$La$_{1.8}$Cu$_{1}$O$_{4}$ and Ba$_{0.1}$La$_{1.9}$Cu$_{1}$O$_{4}$ both
belong to $\{$Ba, La, Cu, O$\}$. This is the grouping used by 3DSC~\cite{sommer2023} and, as the
unit of analysis, by PhaseSelect~\cite{vasylenko2023}; it is inspectable from the formula. It is an
\emph{operational} grouping, not a one-to-one map onto doping series: the single system
$\{$Ba, Cu, O, Y$\}$ holds \num{720} Dataset~A rows in \num{184} distinct formulas with $\Tc$ from
\SI{4.3}{\kelvin} to \SI{130}{\kelvin}, and it merges the structurally distinct
YBa$_2$Cu$_3$O$_{7-\delta}$, Y$_2$Ba$_4$Cu$_7$O$_{15-\delta}$ and YBa$_2$Cu$_4$O$_8$ phases
(\num{596}, \num{38} and \num{51} rows with Cu:Y ratios of \num{3}, \num{3.5} and
\num{4})~\cite{gupta1993}. Holding out a system therefore holds out several related phases at once,
which is a conservative choice for the question of unseen chemistries and a coarse one for the
question of unseen phases; section~\ref{sec:results-levels} compares it with finer and coarser
separations.

The concentration is severe (figure~\ref{fig:anatomy}(a)). Dataset~A has \num{3365} systems for
\num{21263} rows, \SI{79.5}{\percent} of the rows in systems of five or more members, the three
largest holding \num{720}, \num{358} and \num{323} rows, and \num{1169} singletons. Dataset~B has
\num{3046} systems for \num{12336} rows, \SI{66.5}{\percent} in systems of five or more, a largest
system of \num{131} rows and \num{1316} singletons. Labels are assigned with
\texttt{pandas.factorize} \emph{after} all row filtering (section~\ref{sec:repro}).

\subsection{Separation levels and cross-validation protocols}
\label{sec:protocols}

The main comparison is between two five-fold protocols identical in every respect except the
partition: \textbf{random} (\texttt{KFold(5, shuffle=True)}; members of a system routinely fall on
both sides) and \textbf{element-system} (\texttt{GroupKFold(5)} on the system label; every system
in exactly one fold). For the robustness analysis both protocols are repeated with shuffled
splitters over seeds \numrange{0}{4} (\texttt{GroupKFold(n\_splits=5, shuffle=True,
random\_state=s)}, available since scikit-learn~1.6), so the grouped partition varies with the seed
and not only the model initialisation.

Because ``unseen'' admits several definitions, section~\ref{sec:results-levels} embeds the two
protocols in a ladder of five separation levels, all \texttt{GroupKFold(5)} with a different key
and therefore the same test-set size: \textbf{L0} rows (the random protocol); \textbf{L1}
normalised composition (fractions rounded to $10^{-3}$, so replicate measurements of one
composition never cross folds); \textbf{L2} element system (the grouped protocol);
\textbf{L3} host system, the set of elements with molar fraction at least \num{0.05} (dopants
below that threshold are ignored; \num{0.02} and \num{0.10} as sensitivity); \textbf{L4}
leave-clusters-out, \texttt{KMeans(50)} on the standardised presence-plus-fraction representation,
in the spirit of Meredig \etal~\cite{meredig2018}.

\subsection{Models}
\label{sec:models}

We use the two tree ensembles that dominate reported results on this task, with hyperparameters
fixed per dataset from prior grid searches and held constant across protocols. Dataset~A:
XGBoost~\cite{chen2016} with \num{250} estimators, maximum depth \num{8}, learning rate \num{0.07},
subsample \num{0.9}, column subsample \num{0.7}, \texttt{gamma} \num{0}, \texttt{reg\_alpha}
\num{0.01}; random forest with \num{150} trees, maximum depth \num{25} and \texttt{sqrt} feature
subsampling. Dataset~B: XGBoost with \num{300} estimators, maximum depth \num{10}, learning rate
\num{0.05}, subsample and column subsample \num{0.8}, \texttt{hist} tree method; random forest with
\num{150} trees and maximum depth \num{30}, without feature subsampling. Both forests use
\texttt{min\_samples\_leaf} \num{3} and \texttt{min\_samples\_split} \num{5}. Both configurations
were selected under \emph{random} $K$-fold cross-validation in earlier work; because that could
bias the measured gap, section~\ref{sec:results-tuning} repeats the comparison with
hyperparameters selected by nested cross-validation in \emph{both} arms and over a \num{16}-point
grid. Regression metrics (MAE, RMSE, $\Rsq$) are computed on pooled out-of-fold predictions.

\subsection{Representations and nulls}
\label{sec:nulls}

To separate what the descriptors add from what a label already provides, we evaluate, on identical
folds under both protocols: a \textbf{family-mean null} (no features; predict the training-fold
mean $\Tc$ of the row's system, falling back to the global training mean for an unseen system);
and the same XGBoost on four representations---\textbf{R0} binary element presence
(\num{86} indicators for~A, \num{83} for~B), \textbf{R1} presence plus molar fractions
(\num{172} and \num{166} columns), \textbf{R2} the published descriptors (\num{81} and \num{132}),
and \textbf{R3} descriptors plus fractions (\num{253} and \num{298}). Hyperparameters are not
re-tuned per representation. Two model-class controls accompany them: a five-nearest-neighbour
regressor on R1 and an extra-trees ensemble of \num{500} trees on R2. Differences between
representations are reported as differences of losses ($\Delta$MAE, $\Delta\Rsq$ and the relative
reduction of the sum of squared errors, $1 - \mathrm{SSE}_b/\mathrm{SSE}_a$) with paired intervals;
we do not decompose $\Rsq$ additively, since $\Rsq$ values of nested models are not shares of a
common total.

\subsection{Clustering descriptors and the design effect}
\label{sec:cluster}

With $N$ rows in $k$ systems of sizes $n_i$, a one-way random-effects ANOVA gives the intraclass
correlation
\begin{equation}
\ICC = \frac{\mathrm{MS_B} - \mathrm{MS_W}}{\mathrm{MS_B} + (m_0 - 1)\,\mathrm{MS_W}},
\qquad
m_0 = \frac{N - \sum_i n_i^2 / N}{k - 1},
\label{eq:icc}
\end{equation}
and the in-sample share of $\Tc$ variance attributable to system identity is
$\eta^2 = 1 - \mathrm{SS_{within}}/\mathrm{SS_{total}}$, computed from system means over the full
dataset. $\eta^2$ is not a cross-validated score: it uses every system's mean, including systems a
cross-validated model never sees, and it is not commensurable with any $\Rsq$ in either direction.
We never take differences or ratios between $\eta^2$ and an $\Rsq$, and draw it hatched.

A design effect is defined only for a specified estimator. Under a random-intercept model with
independent systems, common marginal variance $\sigma^2$ and within-system correlation $\rho$, the
variance of the \emph{row-weighted} mean of $\Tc$ is
$\sigma^2 N^{-2}\,[(1-\rho)N + \rho\sum_i n_i^2]$, so
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{deff}_{\mathrm{rows}} = 1 + \rho\,(m_w - 1), \qquad m_w = \sum_i n_i^2 / N,
\qquad \Neff = N/\mathrm{deff},
\label{eq:deff}
\end{equation}
whereas for the \emph{mean of system means} (rows weighted $1/n_i$)
$\mathrm{deff}_{\mathrm{sys}} = (N/k^2)\sum_i[\rho + (1-\rho)/n_i]$. Neither equals
$1 + \rho(m_0 - 1)$, which the previous version of this paper used and which is not the variance
of any estimator of the mean under this model. We report both estimators and state which one a
number refers to; none of them is the effective sample size of the learning problem or of a
predictor's MAE.

\subsection{Selection metric}
\label{sec:screening}

Discovery campaigns rank candidates, so we also score selection. A test row is a positive if its
$\Tc$ is at least the 90th percentile of the \emph{training-fold} $\Tc$ (operator $\ge$; ties at
the threshold occur), so no test information enters the criterion. The prevalence of positives in a
test fold is then close to, but not equal to, \SI{10}{\percent}: under the element-system protocol
whole high-$\Tc$ systems land in one fold and the prevalence ranges from \SI{3.6}{\percent} to
\SI{18.2}{\percent} across the five Dataset~A folds and from \SI{6.3}{\percent} to
\SI{13.5}{\percent} in Dataset~B. The expected precision of a random selector, and of any ranker
whose predictions are constant within a fold, is that fold's prevalence---not \SI{10}{\percent}.
For $K = \num{100}$ we report precision, recall and enrichment (precision over prevalence) as
\emph{fractional} quantities under ties (a tied group contributes its expected number of positives),
the number of distinct systems among the selected rows, the precision under a budget of at most
three rows per system, and, for Dataset~B, the precision when the \num{3925} measured
non-superconductors of the Stanev list ($\Tc \le 0$, \SI{24.1}{\percent} of the enlarged pool) are
admitted to the candidate pool as scored negatives. Intervals are family bootstraps of the test
pools, paired across rankers; per-fold standard deviations are also given.

\subsection{Inference}
\label{sec:inference}

All intervals in this paper are \SI{95}{\percent} percentile intervals from a bootstrap of
\emph{whole element systems} (\num{2000} resamples, seed~0), \emph{paired} between the conditions
being compared (the same resampled systems enter both arms) and conditional on the fitted
out-of-fold predictions; they therefore describe uncertainty over systems, not over refitting,
which the seed analysis of section~\ref{sec:results-robust} covers. Row-resampled intervals, which
the previous version used, are \numrange{1.5}{5.6} times narrower for the same statistics because they
treat replicates of one system as independent. Alongside the pooled MAE we report the
\emph{macro} MAE---the mean over systems of the per-system MAE---so that the gap can be read both
per row and per system.

\subsection{Nearest-neighbour diagnostic}

To confirm that the protocols differ in the way the construction predicts, we measure the Euclidean
distance from each test row to its nearest training row in descriptor space standardised with a
scaler fitted on the \emph{training} rows only, under a random $80/20$ hold-out and a grouped
hold-out of the same size.

\subsection{Temporal corpus}
\label{sec:temporal-data}

For the forward-in-time test we rebuilt a corpus from the primary SuperCon export (\num{26321}
reports; not redistributed, see Data availability). Each report was dated by the year in its
bibliographic field (\SI{99.7}{\percent} of reports; the year of the \emph{report}, not of
discovery; fallback rules for the rest), the chemical exclusions of section~\ref{sec:audit} were
applied to the exact formulas, reports with $\Tc \le 0$ were dropped, and the \num{20771} dated
reports were collapsed to \num{12543} normalised compositions in \num{3267} element systems, each
dated by its first report and labelled with the median $\Tc$ of its reports (maximum as
sensitivity). Compositions are featurized with the Dataset-B recipe and scored with the Dataset-B
models. For each cut $T$ the models are fitted on compositions first reported before $T$ and
evaluated, frozen, on those first reported at or after $T$; independently, using only the pre-$T$
data, each protocol L0--L4 produces the five-fold out-of-fold estimate it would have produced at
the time.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Reproducing the reported performance}
\label{sec:results-repro}

Under the conventional protocol our models match the published range: pooled five-fold random
cross-validation gives MAE $=\SI{5.23}{\kelvin}$ and $\Rsq = \num{0.928}$ for XGBoost on Dataset~A,
and MAE $=\SI{3.78}{\kelvin}$ with $\Rsq = \num{0.932}$ on Dataset~B (table~\ref{tab:main}). These
are the figures a reader of the literature expects, so what follows is not an artefact of a weak
baseline. The audit of section~\ref{sec:audit} moves the Dataset-B figures by little: relative to
the previous checkpoint (\num{12440} rows) the random-split XGBoost MAE falls from
\SI{3.91}{\kelvin} to \SI{3.78}{\kelvin} and the grouped MAE is unchanged (\SI{6.17}{\kelvin} to
\SI{6.18}{\kelvin}), so the XGBoost inflation rises from \SI{57.6}{\percent} to
\SI{63.7}{\percent}; for the random forest it rises from \SI{49.9}{\percent} to
\SI{53.0}{\percent}. Part of that shift is partition noise (section~\ref{sec:audit}), and the
intervals of section~\ref{sec:results-gap} cover both values.

\subsection{Holding out element systems inflates the error}
\label{sec:results-gap}

Enforcing system-disjoint folds raises XGBoost's pooled MAE from \SI{5.23}{\kelvin} to
\SI{8.31}{\kelvin} on Dataset~A ($\Delta$MAE $=\SI{3.08}{\kelvin}$ \ci{2.58}{3.65}, inflation
\SI{58.9}{\percent} \ci{50.4}{68.6}) and from \SI{3.78}{\kelvin} to \SI{6.18}{\kelvin} on
Dataset~B (\SI{2.41}{\kelvin} \ci{2.01}{2.88}; \SI{63.7}{\percent} \ci{54.6}{74.3}), and lowers
$\Rsq$ by \num{0.074} \ci{0.058}{0.094} and \num{0.084} \ci{0.066}{0.109}. The random forest
behaves alike (\SI{61.4}{\percent} \ci{52.2}{71.5} and \SI{53.0}{\percent} \ci{44.5}{62.8}). All
intervals are paired family bootstraps and none approaches zero. Read per system rather than per
row, the gap is about half as large: the macro MAE of XGBoost rises from \SI{5.07}{\kelvin} to
\SI{6.43}{\kelvin} on~A (\SI{27.0}{\percent} \ci{23.8}{30.5}) and from \SI{3.50}{\kelvin} to
\SI{4.53}{\kelvin} on~B (\SI{29.4}{\percent} \ci{25.4}{33.8}); for the random forest
\SI{25.5}{\percent} and \SI{21.9}{\percent}. The pooled inflation is therefore concentrated in the
large systems, whose many rows dominate the pooled average and whose replicates are exactly what a
random split lets the model see (figure~\ref{fig:summary}(a),(b)). That the two curations agree is
consistent with the gap being a property of the task rather than of one pipeline, but since they
share \SI{96.6}{\percent} of their systems (section~\ref{sec:overlap}) it is not an independent
replication.

The nearest-neighbour diagnostic confirms the mechanism (figures~\ref{fig:anatomy}(d)
and~\ref{fig:summary}(d)). Under a random hold-out the median distance from a test row to its
closest training row is \num{0.054} in Dataset~A; under a grouped hold-out of the same size it is
\num{1.26}, a factor of \num{23}. For Dataset~B the medians are \num{0.149} and \num{1.51}, a
factor of \num{10}. Counting directly, \SI{37.0}{\percent} of Dataset~A test rows under five-fold
random splitting have a training row with an \emph{identical} \num{81}-feature vector; under
system-aware splitting the fraction is \SI{0.0}{\percent}. For more than a third of its test set the
random protocol is evaluating exact-duplicate recall. Among the \num{8511} Dataset~A rows that share
their feature vector with at least one other row (\num{2418} groups), the mean absolute deviation
of $\Tc$ from the group median is \SI{3.22}{\kelvin} (\SI{3.49}{\kelvin} from the group mean). We
call this the \emph{dispersion not explained by the representation}: it is variation that no
deterministic model of these descriptors can reproduce on those rows, but it is neither a lower
bound on the population MAE nor pure measurement error, because it uses all labels of the group and
may reflect structure, pressure, preparation or unrecorded conditions. A replicate-excluded
predictor (the median of the \emph{other} replicates of the group) reaches MAE \SI{4.72}{\kelvin}
on these rows, against \SI{4.83}{\kelvin} for XGBoost under random splitting and
\SI{7.81}{\kelvin} under grouped splitting.

Two sensitivity checks bound the measurement. Removing the \num{1927} Dataset~A rows with
coefficient-less oxygen (section~\ref{sec:audit}; \num{19336} rows and \num{3171} systems remain,
folds redrawn) leaves the XGBoost inflation at \SI{61.9}{\percent} \ci{52.8}{72.1} and the
$\Rsq$ drop at \num{0.078}, against \SI{58.9}{\percent} and \num{0.074} on the full table; the
random-forest inflation moves from \SI{61.4}{\percent} to \SI{57.9}{\percent} \ci{48.3}{69.3}.
Second, in the common Magpie space a model trained on all of Dataset~B predicts the Dataset~A rows
whose system \emph{is} in~B with $\Rsq = \num{0.923}$ and the \num{1535} rows whose system is not
with $\Rsq = \num{0.500}$ \ci{0.413}{0.570} (MAE \SI{17.68}{\kelvin}); a model trained on all of~A
predicts the \num{416} Dataset-B rows in systems absent from~A with $\Rsq = \num{0.615}$
\ci{0.415}{0.694} (MAE \SI{3.73}{\kelvin}) and the rest with $\Rsq = \num{0.980}$. The
high in-system values are not generalisation---the same formulas are in both tables---and the
complement values are provisional because the complements are small, unrepresentative
(section~\ref{sec:overlap}) and, for~A, dominated by the oxygen defect; they point the same way as
the grouped protocol.

\subsection{The gap depends on model complexity, not on how the model was tuned}
\label{sec:results-tuning}

The hyperparameters of section~\ref{sec:models} were chosen under random cross-validation, so the
gap could in principle be an artefact of tuning under the leaky protocol. Table~\ref{tab:tuning}
tests this in two blocks that keep the two arms symmetric. In the first the \emph{same} fixed
configuration is scored under both protocols (the published one, and the configuration most often
selected by grouped nested search). In the second a nested search over a \num{16}-point XGBoost
grid (maximum depth $\{4,6,8,10\}$, learning rate $\{0.05,0.1\}$, minimum child weight
$\{1,10\}$; \num{300} estimators) is run inside \emph{each} arm: inner three-fold
\texttt{GroupKFold} within each outer grouped fold, inner three-fold \texttt{KFold} within each
outer random fold. Tuning without leakage does not close the gap: with nested selection in both
arms the inflation is \SI{65.6}{\percent} \ci{56.2}{75.8} on~A and \SI{64.4}{\percent}
\ci{55.4}{74.8} on~B, indistinguishable from the fixed-configuration values. (The previous version
of this paper compared a nested grouped arm with a fixed random arm; that mixed comparison is not a
same-hyperparameter comparison and is withdrawn.)

A related question is whether the gap depends on where the largest system lands. The
$\{$Ba, Cu, O, Y$\}$ system holds \num{720} Dataset~A rows, \SI{64}{\percent} of them above

\SIrange[range-phrase=--,range-units=single]{54.4}{57.2}{\percent} and
\SIrange[range-phrase=--,range-units=single]{60.3}{62.0}{\percent} for the unstratified
grouped splitter with the same seeds. The pooled gap does not depend on the placement of the
largest system; what changes is the error of the single fold that receives it.

\subsection{Separation levels define different target populations}
\label{sec:results-levels}

Table~\ref{tab:levels} embeds the two protocols in the ladder of section~\ref{sec:protocols}. The
levels are not nested. Grouping by normalised composition (L1) removes every exact replicate from
the training side but leaves \SI{88.1}{\percent} (A) and \SI{86.1}{\percent} (B) of test rows with
their element system represented in training, and it costs almost nothing:
$\Delta$MAE $=\SI{+0.24}{\kelvin}$ \ci{+0.12}{+0.36} on~A and \SI{+0.08}{\kelvin} \ci{+0.02}{+0.15}
on~B. Grouping by element system (L2) removes all of them and costs \SI{3.08}{\kelvin} and
\SI{2.41}{\kelvin}. Grouping by host system (L3, dopants below \num{0.05} molar fraction ignored)
is stricter in one direction and looser in another: no test row shares its host with training, but
\SI{50.9}{\percent} (A) and \SI{41.8}{\percent} (B) of test rows again share their full element
system with a training row, because a system that differs only in a trace dopant is a different
system but the same host; L3 withdraws whole classes (all doped variants of one host) at once and
costs \SI{3.72}{\kelvin} and \SI{3.35}{\kelvin}. Leave-clusters-out on composition (L4) is the
most demanding in error (\SI{+5.65}{\kelvin} and \SI{+5.30}{\kelvin}) and in neighbour distance,
yet \SI{12.6}{\percent} and \SI{13.2}{\percent} of its test rows share their element system with
training. Each level therefore answers a different question---new measurements of known
compositions (L0/L1), new stoichiometries of known systems (L1), unseen systems (L2), unseen hosts
(L3), unseen composition regions (L4)---and the strictest is not ``the correct one'' by default;
section~\ref{sec:temporal} asks which of them anticipates what actually happens over time. The
sensitivity of L3 to its threshold is large (A: MAE \SI{8.47}{\kelvin} at \num{0.02},
\SI{10.73}{\kelvin} at \num{0.10}; B: \SI{6.64}{\kelvin} and \SI{7.92}{\kelvin}), which is a
further reason not to treat any single grouping as canonical.

\subsection{What the conventional \texorpdfstring{$\Rsq$}{R2} rewards}
\label{sec:results-ceiling}

\paragraph{The corpus as clustered data.} Treating system identity as a clustering variable, the
intraclass correlation of $\Tc$ is \num{0.854} (A) and \num{0.846} (B), and system identity alone
accounts in-sample for \SI{87.7}{\percent} and \SI{88.4}{\percent} of the variance of $\Tc$
($\eta^2$; table~\ref{tab:deff}, figure~\ref{fig:ceiling}(a)). We report these as descriptions of
how concentrated the target is within systems. The design effect of the row-weighted mean is
$1 + \rho(m_w - 1)$ with $m_w = \num{69.6}$ (A) and \num{17.8} (B)---not Searle's $m_0$ of
\num{6.30} and \num{4.05}---so its effective sample size is \num{357} and \num{809}, rising to
\num{509} and \num{853} when the largest system is removed: the row-weighted estimator is dominated
by the largest systems. For the mean of system means the effective size is \num{3621} and
\num{3249}, close to $k$. These numbers describe the precision of one estimator of the mean $\Tc$
under one model (figure~\ref{fig:ceiling}(b)); they are not the effective sample size of the
learning problem or of a predictor's MAE, and we withdraw the previous claim that the datasets carry
``the information of $k$ independent observations''.

\paragraph{Relatives versus training-set size.} Removing the same-system relatives of the test rows
from the training pool shrinks the pool, so part of the grouped penalty could be a sample-size
effect. Two designs separate the two. With a random test fold (D1), removing all relatives leaves
\num{3707} training rows on~A and raises the MAE by \SI{4.90}{\kelvin} \ci{4.22}{5.63}; removing
the same number of rows at random raises it by \SI{1.58}{\kelvin} \ci{1.45}{1.71}, so
\SI{67.9}{\percent} of the penalty is attributable to the relatives (B: \SI{62.6}{\percent}). With
half of each grouped fold as test set (D2), the relatives are only \SI{10.1}{\percent} of the pool,
removing them costs \SI{2.58}{\kelvin} \ci{2.08}{3.14} and removing as many random rows costs
\SI{0.07}{\kelvin} \ci{0.01}{0.12}: \SI{97.4}{\percent} of the penalty is the relatives (B:
\SI{95.1}{\percent}). We report both designs; the answer depends on how many rows the relatives
represent, and in neither is the grouped penalty a training-size artefact.

\subsection{Selection on held-out systems}
\label{sec:results-screening}

Table~\ref{tab:screen} scores the selection task with the corrected metric of
section~\ref{sec:screening}. Under random splits the featureless family-mean null selects
\SI{95.8}{\percent} positives on~A---a lookup of the test row's own system is a strong ranker when
its replicates are in training---and the descriptor models reach \SI{98.4}{\percent}. Under
element-system hold-out the null's precision equals the fold prevalence by construction
(\SI{10.3}{\percent} and \SI{10.2}{\percent}; the previous version reported \SI{11.6}{\percent} and
\SI{9.6}{\percent}, which were artefacts of how ties were broken among a constant prediction), while
XGBoost retains \SI{87.0}{\percent} \ci{77.2}{92.0} on~A and \SI{82.4}{\percent} \ci{75.1}{88.8}
on~B, an enrichment of \num{10.7} and \num{8.6} over prevalence and a recall of about a quarter
to a third of all positives with \num{100} picks. This is \emph{retrospective precision among corpus
materials whose systems were held out}: every candidate is a known superconductor, so the number
says nothing about the yield of a synthesis campaign. Admitting the \num{3925} measured
non-superconductors of the Stanev list to the Dataset-B candidate pool lowers the grouped XGBoost
precision from \SI{82.4}{\percent} to \SI{78.2}{\percent} \ci{70.0}{84.8}, with \num{6.2}
\ci{3.8}{8.8} non-superconductors expected among the \num{100} picks. The selected sets are also
concentrated: \num{19} distinct systems among \num{100} picks on~A, and a budget of at most three
rows per system lowers the precision to \SI{74.2}{\percent}.

How much of the selection skill is due to the descriptors rather than to element identity? Element
presence alone reaches \SI{84.3}{\percent} and \SI{68.0}{\percent} on held-out systems. The paired
difference XGBoost minus presence is \num{+2.7} percentage points \ci{-0.8}{+14.2} on~A---not
distinguishable from zero---and \num{+14.4} points \ci{+6.7}{+24.5} on~B. The previous version's
``$7.7 \pm 1.0$ points'' for~A was an across-seed dispersion, not an uncertainty over systems, and
is withdrawn. Whether the descriptors contribute selection skill beyond element identity therefore
depends on the dataset, and on~A the evidence is inconclusive.

\subsection{Which retrospective protocol anticipates performance on later superconductors?}
\label{sec:temporal}

Cross-validation protocols are proxies for a question that can be asked directly of SuperCon: how
well does a model trained on everything known up to a year $T$ predict the superconductors first
reported at or after $T$? On the dated corpus of section~\ref{sec:temporal-data}, between
\SI{72}{\percent} and \SI{84}{\percent} of the compositions reported after each cut belong to
element systems that did not exist in the database before it, so the forward-in-time test is
dominated by the regime that grouped validation is meant to emulate.

The realised performance is far below the random-CV figure at every cut (table~\ref{tab:temporal},
figure~\ref{fig:temporal}). At the principal cut $T$ = 2010 (\num{10215} training compositions,
\num{2328} later ones, \SI{76}{\percent} in new systems) XGBoost reaches MAE \SI{7.43}{\kelvin}
\ci{6.67}{8.27} and $\Rsq = \num{0.33}$ \ci{0.18}{0.47}; on the later compositions from \emph{new}
systems $\Rsq$ is \num{0.19} \ci{0.01}{0.34} (MAE \SI{8.47}{\kelvin}), on those from systems already
seen it is \num{0.83} \ci{0.71}{0.89} (MAE \SI{4.07}{\kelvin}), and on later re-measurements of
compositions already in the training set the model recovers MAE \SI{2.39}{\kelvin} and
$\Rsq = \num{0.80}$. Random five-fold CV on the same pre-2010 data estimates MAE \SI{4.34}{\kelvin}
and $\Rsq = \num{0.91}$: it understates the realised error by \SI{3.10}{\kelvin} \ci{2.25}{4.03}
and overstates $\Rsq$ by \num{0.58} \ci{0.44}{0.73}. The $\Rsq$ overstatement of random CV is
significant at all four cuts (\num{+0.28}, \num{+0.60}, \num{+0.58} and \num{+0.42} at 1995,
2005, 2010 and 2015); grouping by composition (L1) changes nothing, because the corpus holds one row
per composition. Grouping by element system (L2) or by host (L3) moves the MAE estimate into the
realised interval at 2005 and 2010 ($\Delta$MAE $=\SI{-0.67}{\kelvin}$ \ci{-1.83}{+0.43} and
\SI{+0.10}{\kelvin} \ci{-1.00}{+1.12} at 2010) but reduces the $\Rsq$ overstatement only
marginally (\num{+0.50} and \num{+0.47} at 2010). Leave-clusters-out (L4) is the only protocol
whose $\Rsq$ estimate matches the realised value at 1995 ($\Delta\Rsq = \num{-0.05}$
\ci{-0.12}{+0.02}) and 2015 (\num{+0.11} \ci{-0.16}{+0.29}), but it is pessimistic in MAE after
2005 (\SI{+3.92}{\kelvin} \ci{2.55}{5.17} at 2010) and still overstates $\Rsq$ by
\numrange{0.28}{0.30} at 2005--2010. Averaged over the four cuts, the mean absolute discrepancy
in $\Rsq$ falls monotonically with the strength of the grouping (L0 \num{0.47}, L2 \num{0.39}, L3
\num{0.35}, L4 \num{0.19}), while in MAE the host protocol is closest (L3 \SI{2.2}{\kelvin}; L0
\SI{3.8}{\kelvin}; L4 \SI{3.5}{\kelvin}). Re-weighting the random-CV out-of-fold rows to the
new/seen-system mix of the later compositions does not recover the realised value either ($\Rsq$
\num{0.81} versus \num{0.33} at 2010): the out-of-fold rows from new systems inside a random split
are not representative of the systems that are actually discovered later.

Two observations bound the comparison. First, the screening metric cannot be transferred across
time as a precision: the prevalence of compositions above the training 90th percentile among the
later compositions collapses from \SI{9.2}{\percent} at 1995 to \SIrange{0.9}{1.8}{\percent} at
2005--2015, so the realised top-\num{100} precision falls from \num{0.90} to \numrange{0.08}{0.47}
while every protocol estimates \numrange{0.36}{0.99}; as a lift over prevalence the
forward-in-time ranking is strong (\numrange{18}{26} times at 2005--2010), and the Spearman rank
correlation degrades more gently (\num{0.95} retrospective versus \numrange{0.57}{0.78} realised).
Second, the realised $\Rsq$ is fragile: removing the \num{526} training compositions whose year came
from a fallback dating rule (\SI{5}{\percent} of the training set, mostly 1960s--1970s alloys and
Chevrel phases) shifts the XGBoost predictions for the then-unseen Bi--S--O family from about
\SI{15}{\kelvin} to about \SI{60}{\kelvin} and drives the realised $\Rsq$ at 2010 from \num{0.33}
to \num{-0.20} (MAE \SI{7.4}{\kelvin} to \SI{9.1}{\kelvin}), whereas the random-CV and
leave-clusters-out estimates move by less than \SI{0.3}{\kelvin} in MAE and \num{0.02} in $\Rsq$.
Using the maximum instead of the median $\Tc$ per composition, or re-seeding the random and cluster
partitions, leaves all conclusions unchanged (\texttt{results\_v3/temporal\_validation.md}).

\emph{Answer to the question.} No retrospective protocol reproduces the forward-in-time
performance. Random cross-validation is the worst proxy for $\Rsq$ at every cut and for MAE at 1995
and 2005; at 2010 the worst MAE proxy is leave-clusters-out (\SI{+3.9}{\kelvin} against
\SI{-3.1}{\kelvin} for random CV), at 2015 random CV is closest in MAE because the later
compositions are low-$\Tc$ and low-variance, and at 1995 all protocols agree within their intervals
on top-\num{100} precision. The grouped protocols are consistently closer, with the host grouping
(L3) the best predictor of the realised MAE and leave-clusters-out (L4) the best predictor of the
realised $\Rsq$, and the direction of their residual error is stable (random CV optimistic, LOCO
pessimistic in MAE after 2005). The realised $\Rsq$ on new element systems
(\numrange{0.1}{0.5}) is the number a user of these models should expect, and none of the
protocols, applied to the data available at the time, would have predicted it to within \num{0.1}
at more than one cut.

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{What the numbers mean}

Superconductor datasets are clustered samples of doping series, not simple random samples of
compounds, and every consequence measured here follows from that. Row-level splitting scores
interpolation within a series the model has already seen: it rewards proximity to a replicate, which
is why a featureless lookup of the system mean ranks almost as well as the descriptor model
(table~\ref{tab:screen}), why a five-nearest-neighbour regressor on presence plus fractions matches
the tree ensemble (table~\ref{tab:ablation}), and why the pooled inflation on unseen systems is
concentrated in the large systems (figure~\ref{fig:summary}(b)). The clustering has a name outside
materials informatics---cluster sampling---and the descriptive quantities that go with it (family
concentration, intraclass correlation) are reported here as descriptions; a design effect, we have
learned, is only defined once the estimator is named, and the effective sample size of ``the
dataset'' is not a well-posed quantity.

The forward-in-time test answers the question the paper is organised around. Grouped protocols are
better proxies for performance on later superconductors than random cross-validation, in MAE and in
$\Rsq$, and the more the grouping removes---composition, element system, host, composition
cluster---the smaller the $\Rsq$ overstatement becomes. But none of them reproduces the realised
value, the best proxy differs by metric and by cut, and the realised $\Rsq$ itself is fragile to how
a few per cent of the training compositions are dated. The practical reading is that a grouped
protocol should be reported \emph{as a proxy with a stated direction of error}, not as the
extrapolation accuracy, and that the number a user should expect on genuinely new element systems is
$\Rsq$ of order \numrange{0.1}{0.5}, not \num{0.9}.

\subsection{Relation to prior work}
\label{sec:prior}

Table~\ref{tab:prior} places this work against the studies that bear directly on it. The two source
papers of our datasets validate at random~\cite{hamidieh2018,stanev2018}; Stanev \etal\ also report
that a model trained on one macro-family has no predictive power on another, but their headline
figure is the random one. Meredig \etal~\cite{meredig2018} introduced leave-one-cluster-out
validation and showed that extrapolative performance can be far below the interpolative figure; we
rely on their abstract and on secondary descriptions, since we could not obtain the full text, and
attribute no procedural detail to them beyond that. Xiong \etal~\cite{xiong2020} order folds by
the property value instead. Sheridan~\cite{sheridan2013} compared random, time-split and
leave-class-out validation against realised prospective $\Rsq$ in QSAR and found the random figure
too optimistic and the class-held-out figure too pessimistic; section~\ref{sec:temporal} is the
same design on SuperCon, and reaches a compatible conclusion. Roberts \etal~\cite{roberts2017}
argue for blocked cross-validation with the caution that blocking can induce extrapolation and
overestimate the interpolative error, which is the caution our separation ladder makes concrete.

SuperCon is very clustered, without comparing grouped with random validation;
PhaseSelect~\cite{vasylenko2023} takes the element set as the unit of analysis and thereby discards
the stoichiometric information whose contribution we measure. What this paper adds is the
quantification of the gap on identical rows, models and budgets, with family-paired uncertainty;
the null and representation ladders; the audit of the chemical unit and of the overlap between
curations; and the forward-in-time scoring of the protocols themselves.

\subsection{Scope and limitations}

The family-mean null's $\Rsq \approx 0$ under grouped validation is a calibration, not a finding:
with every test system unseen the lookup returns the training-fold mean, and its pooled $\Rsq$ is
slightly negative only because that mean differs from the test-fold mean. The oracle $\eta^2$ is an
in-sample descriptive quantity; the random-split $\Rsq$ of \num{0.928} exceeds it, which is itself a
symptom of the leakage measured here, and we never combine it arithmetically with a cross-validated
value.

The two curations are not independent (section~\ref{sec:overlap}); their agreement bounds the
sensitivity of the result to curation and featurization, not to the underlying corpus. Dataset~A is
used as distributed and carries the oxygen defect of section~\ref{sec:audit}, whose effect on the
gap we could only bound by row removal. The complement cross-evaluation and the forward-in-time test
are the only evaluations here whose test materials are not also in the training source, and both
have small or unrepresentative test sets at some cuts. The temporal corpus is dated by the year of
the SuperCon report, not of discovery, \SI{23}{\percent} of its compositions have several dated
reports (the first is used), and a fallback dating rule affects \num{570} compositions; the
sensitivity to those rows is large for $\Rsq$ and small for MAE. We have measured only tree
ensembles, a $k$-nearest-neighbour control and extra trees; whether the gap widens for graph and
deep composition models is not measured here. The selection metric is retrospective throughout: a
prospective campaign with a pre-registered candidate list would be the decisive test of the
screening claim, and this paper does not make it.

\subsection{Reproducibility}
\label{sec:repro}

The pipeline code, the audited Dataset-B checkpoint with its row-level audit table, the result
tables underlying every table and figure, and the scripts that render the figures from those
tables are released together (Data availability). Dataset~A is redistributed by UCI and fetched by a
script. The partitions are released as data: for every row of both datasets, its system identifier,
its fold under the deterministic \texttt{GroupKFold(5)}, its fold under shuffled \texttt{GroupKFold}
for seeds \numrange{0}{4}, and its fold under \texttt{KFold} for the same seeds. Because
\texttt{GroupKFold} breaks ties by label order, system labels must be factorised after row
filtering; the previous release factorised before removing the $\Tc \le 0$ rows and therefore
evaluated a different partition from the one it distributed, and the audit changes the partition
again (section~\ref{sec:audit}). Top-\num{100} precision is sensitive to the XGBoost version, so we
pin \texttt{xgboost==3.0.5}. Every number in this text is checked \emph{in its own location}
(table cell or sentence) against the result file it comes from by
\texttt{code/v3/verify\_manuscript.py}; the previous verifier checked only that each number appeared
somewhere in the file and accepted a corrupted table cell, and a mutation test now confirms that
each of a set of injected corruptions is detected. The figures reported here were produced with
Python~3.13, numpy~2.3.3, scikit-learn~1.7.2, xgboost~3.0.5 and matminer~0.9.2.

\section{Conclusion}

Composition-based $\Tc$ models are evaluated on datasets built from doping series, and splitting
those datasets at the level of the row measures interpolation within a series rather than
generalisation to a new chemistry. Holding out whole element systems inflates the pooled MAE by
\SIrange{53}{64}{\percent} and lowers $\Rsq$ by \numrange{0.074}{0.084} in two curations of
SuperCon and two featurizations, with paired family intervals that exclude zero; per system the
inflation is \SIrange{22}{29}{\percent}. Most of what the conventional $\Rsq$ rewards is available
from element identity, most of the remainder from stoichiometry, and on unseen systems the physical
descriptors do not beat element presence plus fractions. Retrospective selection precision among
held-out corpus materials remains high (\SI{87.0}{\percent} and \SI{82.4}{\percent}), but it is a
statement about ranking known superconductors, not about synthesis yield, and on Dataset~A the
contribution of the descriptors to it is not distinguishable from zero.

The evaluation that comes closest to a prospective test---training on everything reported before a
year and scoring what was reported afterwards---is anticipated by none of the retrospective
protocols: random cross-validation overstates $\Rsq$ by \numrange{0.28}{0.60}, grouped protocols
are closer and leave-clusters-out closest in $\Rsq$, and the realised $\Rsq$ on new element systems
is \numrange{0.1}{0.5}. We therefore recommend reporting a grouped protocol alongside the random
one, stating which target population it stands for, and treating its figure as a proxy with a known
direction of error. The corrected protocols cost one additional cross-validation run each, and the
released partitions, audit and verifier make them one command.
