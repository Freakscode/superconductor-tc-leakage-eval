# Proposed figure captions — manuscript v2 (English)

Format follows paper 2: a bold lead sentence stating the takeaway, then (a)/(b)/(c)/(d).
Every caption declares *n*, the top-100 threshold definition where it applies, and which
quantities are oracle versus cross-validated.

All figures are regenerated by `results_v2/make_figures.py`, which reads the canonical
measurements in `results_v2/` and recomputes only the panels that are not tabulated there
(family-size distribution, parity predictions, nearest-neighbour distances). The script
ends in 81 fidelity asserts that compare every drawn value against its source CSV.

Throughout: **top-100 screening precision** is the fraction of the 100 highest-*predicted*
materials in a test fold whose true *T*<sub>c</sub> lies at or above the 90th percentile of
the *training* fold's *T*<sub>c</sub> (`np.percentile(y_train, 90)`), averaged over the 5
folds. Computing the threshold on the training fold only is what keeps the test labels out
of the threshold; the 10% base rate follows from that definition.

Also throughout: the oracle η² of Figure 4a is an **in-sample** quantity and every *R*² is
**out-of-sample** (grouped CV). No ratio or difference is formed between the two anywhere in
these captions. Differences are quoted only between quantities that share a protocol.

## Figure 1 — `figure1.png`

**Chemical data leakage in the Hamidieh dataset is structural, not incidental: most
materials share their exact element set with another material, and validation that ignores
this tests models on near-duplicates.** (a) Distribution of chemical-family size, where a
family is the set of materials sharing an identical set of constituent elements
(*n* = 21,263 materials in *k* = 3,365 families; the largest family, {Ba, Cu, O, Y},
contains 720 doping variants). 94.5% of materials sit in a family with at least one other
member. (b) Test MAE under 5-fold cross-validation for both models, comparing a random
split against a chemical-family split in which whole families are held out; bars are means
over 5 independent family partitions (`GroupKFold(5, shuffle=True, random_state=0…4)`),
error bars are the standard deviation across those partitions, and open circles are the
individual partitions. Family-aware validation raises MAE by 57% (XGBoost) and 60%
(RandomForest). (c) Parity plot for XGBoost under chemical-family hold-out, pooling the
out-of-fold predictions of the deterministic `GroupKFold(5)` partition (*n* = 21,263
predictions, each made by a model that never saw the test material's family;
MAE = 8.3 K, *R*² = 0.85 — both cross-validated). (d) Distance from each test material to
its nearest *training* material in the standardized 81-dimensional feature space, for a
single 80/20 hold-out under each scheme (*n* = 4,253 random-split and 4,344
family-split test materials; distances clipped at 3 for display). The median test material
is 23× farther from its nearest training example under the family split (1.27 versus
0.054), which is the mechanism behind the error increase in (b).

## Figure 2 — `figure2.png`

**A featureless predictor that only returns each family's mean *T*<sub>c</sub> reproduces
most of the headline performance under random splitting, which locates that performance in
family memorization rather than in composition–property learning.** (a) Decomposition of
the cross-validated *R*² into the part a featureless family-mean predictor already achieves
(grey) and the increment contributed by the paper's features and model (blue), for both
datasets under both schemes (Dataset A: *n* = 21,263, *k* = 3,365; Dataset B: *n* = 12,440,
*k* = 3,063). Under random splitting the null alone reaches 86% (A) and 82% (B) of the full
model's *R*²; under family-aware splitting it collapses to *R*² ≈ 0 (−0.007 and −0.003,
drawn as a baseline stub) because a held-out family has no training mean to look up. All
values are cross-validated; none is an oracle. (b) Top-100 screening precision of the same
featureless ranker against XGBoost. Under random splitting the null ranks nearly as well as
the model (95.8% versus 98.4% on A); under family-aware splitting it falls to 11.6% (A) and
9.6% (B) — at or near the 10% base rate implied by the top-decile threshold — while XGBoost
retains 87.0% and 85.2%. Threshold as defined above.

## Figure 3 — `figure3.png`

**The chemical-data-leakage gap is not an artifact of one dataset, one featurization or one
model: it replicates across two independently constructed datasets, two model families and
five independent family partitions.** (a) Test MAE and (b) *R*² for each dataset × model
combination, comparing random against chemical-family splitting. Points are means over 5
independent partitions (`GroupKFold(5, shuffle=True, random_state=0…4)` for the grouped arm,
`KFold(5, shuffle=True, random_state=0…4)` for the random arm); the annotation gives the
paired per-seed inflation in MAE and the signed change in *R*². MAE rises 52–61% and *R*²
falls by 0.071–0.081 in all four combinations. All quantities are cross-validated.
(c) Top-100 screening precision under both schemes (threshold as defined above); bars are
the 5-fold mean of the deterministic `GroupKFold(5)` partition, error bars its standard
deviation across folds, and open circles the individual folds. Screening precision remains
87.0% (A) and 85.2% (B) under family-aware validation, so the ranking task degrades far less
than the pointwise-error task. (d) Distance to the nearest training neighbour in
standardized feature space for a single 80/20 hold-out per scheme (A: *n* = 4,253 / 4,344;
B: *n* = 2,488 / 2,845 test materials); boxes show the interquartile range, whiskers the
5th–95th percentile. The median family-split test material is 23× (A) and 8.9× (B) farther
from training data than under random splitting. Dataset A is Hamidieh's 81 compositional
statistics (*n* = 21,263); Dataset B is the Stanev SuperCon list Magpie-featurized with
matminer, restricted to *T*<sub>c</sub> > 0 (*n* = 12,440, 132 features).

## Figure 4 — `figure4.png` (also `figure4_ceiling.png`)

**Chemical-family identity alone explains about 88% of the in-sample variance in
*T*<sub>c</sub>, and the clustering that produces that figure also reduces the effective
sample size to roughly the number of families rather than the number of materials.**
(a) Variance in *T*<sub>c</sub> explained by four quantities, for Dataset A (light) and
Dataset B (dark). The leftmost pair is an **in-sample oracle**: η² from a one-way
random-effects ANOVA using the true per-family mean *T*<sub>c</sub> computed on **all** the
data (0.877 for A, 0.884 for B). It is drawn hatched, in its own axis band, because it is
**not** a cross-validated score and not achievable by any model that has not seen the test
family; it is shown for scale, and no ratio or difference between it and the
cross-validated bars is formed anywhere in this figure or in the text, since an in-sample
and an out-of-sample quantity are not commensurable. The remaining three pairs are all
cross-validated under `GroupKFold(5)` on identical folds: the paper's full features
(0.855 / 0.853), a null trained only on binary element-presence indicators (0.803 / 0.779),
and the featureless family-mean null (−0.007 / −0.003, drawn as a baseline stub). Because
those three share a protocol, they *can* be compared directly: discarding every statistical
and Magpie descriptor and keeping only the list of elements present costs 0.052 of *R*² on A
and 0.074 on B (brackets), so most of the cross-validated performance is attributable to
*which* elements are present rather than to their proportions or to the elemental
descriptors. (b) The same clustering expressed as a design effect. Nominal *N* is the
material count; effective *N* is *N* divided by the design effect 1 + (*m*₀ − 1)·ICC(1)
with Searle's *m*₀ for unequal cluster sizes; *k* is the number of chemical families
(hatched, since it is a count of design units rather than of observations). With
ICC(1) = 0.85 on both datasets, the design effect is 5.5× (A) and 3.6× (B), so 21,263
materials carry about as much independent information as 3,847 — close to the 3,365
families — and 12,440 carry about as much as 3,470, close to 3,063 families. Sample sizes
and error bars computed as if the *N* materials were independent are therefore optimistic
by roughly the square root of the design effect.
